But there’s some good news, too: I won a science fiction short story writing competition! Let’s just say the competition wasn’t exactly fierce, considering only three entries were submitted in total.

The entry had to be written in Hungarian, but the translated version is now available on the site, titled Spacetrouble. Enjoy reading!

Spacetrouble

Life on Mars was tough; terraforming was only the beginning for the new arrivals. First came the astronauts, the scientists, the engineers, the biologists, and the astronomers. All of them were busy trying to make life on the freshly inhabited planet even a tiny bit manageable. It wasn’t just the heavy air of the new atmosphere that felt strange, but the weather was also quite different from what they knew on Earth. Magnetic storms and acidic rains were common, each one making farming and livestock raising much more difficult. Crops grew reluctantly, and the cows weren’t fond of the sour-tasting grass. As a result, they didn’t produce any milk, and this was a problem, because for some reason the settlers really wanted to drink milk.

This, of course, wasn’t a problem for the natives. They knew the climate of their planet well; they knew what would grow in their fields, and they also knew how to make milk. Their milk didn’t require cows at all. They were tiny creatures, but incredibly quick and sharp-witted enough to handle any situation with ease. And perhaps most importantly, they couldn’t stand the newcomers to Mars, since the newcomers had been hostile from the very beginning, even though they only wanted to live side by side in peace.

That cleverness was very much needed by the tiny creature that was now sitting in captivity on the table of Orbital Research Lab VIII in Dome VI. Its freedom was limited by a laser cage and the three plasma locks placed on it. The bluish glow of the laser beams filled the lab, while the plasma’s color was nothing more than a dull white. Its captor, meanwhile, was working on one of his inventions on the other side of the lab; he wanted at least a small moment of triumph, since the milk-synthesizing attempt had once again ended in failure. He was just adding the final touches to the palm-sized gravity generator, the welding torch in his hand throwing sparks everywhere as he worked the seams on its kryptonite casing. Cirr-cirrr, durrrrrr, and pzzzzzzzzzzt, these were the only sounds filling the lab, breaking the silence.

As soon as it was ready, he intended to test what it could do, and of course he would try it out on his tiny prisoner. “This much is acceptable in the name of scientific progress,” he said, laughing loudly. “This much is not acceptable,” his prisoner would have argued if they had been able to communicate, but they couldn’t understand a word the other said.

He spun around on his lab chair, a huge smile spreading across his face, and with great momentum pushed himself toward the prisoner. He held the device out in front of him, its antenna ending in a wobbling platinum sphere, and he pointed it at the cage. Then he pressed the single, comically oversized red button on the device, which activated the superconducting levitation platforms around the cage. Gravity disappeared around the creature, and it began to rise slowly. At first it was frightened by the unusual sensation, but it quickly adapted; thanks to its small body and large round ears, controlling itself in weightlessness didn’t pose much difficulty. In fact, it floated gracefully through the energy gaps of the cage, and then it was already taking to its heels, using its quick thinking to head straight for the lab’s only exit. The scientist immediately reached after it, but the momentum made him spin around his own axis, and he somersaulted forward. This wasn’t surprising, since he, too, was now floating in weightlessness. There was no room for anything besides the big red button on the palm-sized device, so nothing could regulate the size of the anti-gravity field, which kept expanding. Now not only he and his runaway were drifting above the white, acid-resistant laboratory tables, but also the quantum-jump screwdriver, the plasma-vortex wrench, the photosynthetic soldering station, the magnetic level, and three flasks filled with colored liquids. The first contained a red goo, the second a blue sludge, and the last a green slime. The remote control was floating as well, drifting straight toward the ceiling, and while the scientist stared at the receding gadget with an open mouth and wide eyes, the lab chair smacked him in the face. Even in weightlessness, it delivered a solid slap. He snapped out of his daze at once and used the chair as a launching point, shooting up toward the ceiling. Stretching out his arm, he grabbed his invention, pressed the red button, and gravity returned, the satisfied grin on his face lasted only a moment. He fell from the ceiling to the floor, and down came the quantum-jump screwdriver, the plasma-vortex wrench, the photosynthetic soldering station, and the magnetic level as well. PIFF, PAFF, PUFF, and finally BUMM, that’s how the tools landed on his head. The flasks crashed down too; the blue sludge spilled into the red goo, and together they turned into a colorless concoction.

Even though the scientist’s head was throbbing from all the piffs and puffs, he quickly waved away the stars dancing around his eyes and grabbed one of the gadgets lying all over the place, hoping he could still stop the little creature from escaping. He was very lucky, because what he grabbed turned out to be a hologram multiplier module. The single exit suddenly became sixteen, which thoroughly confused the fugitive. Trusting its instincts, it chose the twelfth door from the left. The result was a huge thud as it splattered against the wall. Its body flattened completely, as if someone had rolled over it with a rolling pin. With great difficulty it peeled itself off the wall with a loud squelch, popped its thumb into its mouth, and blew hard. PLOOOOOP! It instantly regained its little Martian shape.

The scientist immediately reached for the next gadget, which happened to be the plasma-vortex generator. One click, and in no time not one, but two tornadoes were swirling around. It was one of the first inventions he had built on Mars, and he was very proud of it, since it could produce two tornadoes at once, at the cost of any real controllability. With no target, one tornado rushed off and picked up most of the junk that had fallen to the floor, while the other made a wide turn and headed straight for the scientist. There was nothing he could do about it now; he straightened his lab coat, folded back his collar, brushed off his shoulder, closed his eyes, and waited for whatever was going to happen. The tornado scooped him up and spun him around. His head faced inward, so no one could see his tears; his backside faced outward, spinning now high, now low in the whirlwind. This was already a problem, but a bigger one was that the other tornado had turned back as well, carrying with it the quantum-jump screwdriver, the plasma-vortex wrench, the photosynthetic soldering station, and the magnetic level. His backside and the tools soon met: PIFF, PAFF, PUFF, and finally BUMM, that’s how the objects thumped against his rear. Everything was thrown out of the two tornadoes; as a farewell, they spun one last victory lap, knocking down every test tube, overturning all the centrifuge vials, and then grabbing the flask with the colorless concoction and shaking it vigorously, turning it into a yellow stuff. The chaos was enormous, but at least nothing was on fire yet.

The holograms finally disappeared, and the way to the door was clear again, so the fugitive scurried off as fast as it could. It leapt over the broken glass containers, squeezed under the tilted chair, and ran triumphantly through the doorway. Before doing so, it even turned back to stick out its tongue and mock the scientist, only to find itself back in the lab again, right in front of the door. Because of its momentum, it shot through the doorway again, still looking back and teasing him exactly the same way. And again, it ended up in the lab, only this time even closer to the scientist. It had no idea what was happening, how could it? It had never seen a teleportation loop in action before. Suddenly, a hole opened under its feet, it dropped straight through it, and fell directly into the scientist’s hand, then felt a strong grip close around its body. What frightened it wasn’t the grip itself, but the huge claws sticking out from the scientist’s hand.

The new destination was the laser cage, but getting there proved to be quite difficult; the scientist had to skillfully dodge the glass shards, the gadgets, and practically every overturned object in the lab. The still-glowing plasma lock was lying on the floor as well, but the scientist didn’t notice it; he stepped on it, burned the sole of his foot, and yelped, and the grip around the tiny body immediately loosened. Not only around the tiny body, but also around the teleportation loop switch he was holding in his other hand, which dropped to the floor, and since it also had a comically oversized red button and landed button-side down, it got pressed. Both of them flew through a loop, knocking over the cryostat, tipping over the fusion columns, and bringing down the plasma generators. The chaos grew even worse, and now something was actually on fire, among other things, the yellow stuff, which, under the heat, turned into white milk.

The destruction was enormous; there wasn’t a single intact surface left on the tables, the walls were covered with the liquids splashed from the test tubes and flasks, oil was dripping from the pneumatic devices, and the scientist lay on the floor, clawing at it in agony, one of his claws even broke off. What would his supervisor say when he saw the lab in ruins? What scolding would he get for the fact that there had been no progress on the milk-synthesizing project for weeks?

He wailed and screamed; his scientific career was surely ruined, and after this no one would trust him with anything again. In the middle of his hysteria, he didn’t notice the flames spreading farther and farther, and one of the tongues of fire set his tail on fire. Yes, his tail caught fire, and the tip began to burn. Oh, did I not mention it? The scientist was a lanky grey house cat named Tom; at least that was what was embroidered on his lab coat. That part was still readable, but the rest of the coat was covered in dirt and grey grime, though at least, the color matched his fur. The little Martian was a small brown mouse; he didn’t have a lab coat with an embroidered name, so I don’t know what his name might have been. But I can’t let a character stay without a name. So let him be, Jerry!

Now the wailing and screaming became truly loud; like the intercontinental rocket they had arrived on when coming to Mars, Tom shot upward from the pain. He bounced between the ceiling and the floor while trying to catch his tail to put out the fire. With great difficulty he finally managed it, choosing the empty spot next to the milk flask as his landing site. When he reached the ground, his jaw dropped in amazement and hit the floor. What sound it made, I leave that to the reader’s imagination.

He couldn’t imagine how it could have formed; for weeks he hadn’t been able to produce it, and yet here it was right in front of him. Jerry didn’t understand what was so interesting about the synthetic milk; after all, it was just a bit of red goo, blue sludge, some shaking, and heating. Soon his own jaw dropped to the floor in amazement as well. Lying on the floor was a lunchbox, and inside it was enormous slice of star-cheese, something the scientist had brought all the way from Earth, made from Earth milk. The Martians only occasionally managed to make cheese; it was considered an incredible rarity and the finest delicacy. Tom, of course, did not understand this amazement, since making cheese from milk was no great feat. They looked at each other, and even without words, they understood one another.

Jerry showed Tom how to make milk, and Tom showed Jerry how to make cheese from milk. They shook hands and became good friends. Tom received the Nobel Prize for his discovery and became a famous scientist (of course, only after he cleaned up the lab). Jerry also received an award (and an unlimited supply of star-cheese) for creating peace between the two species. They realized that together they could get much further than they ever could alone, and from that day on everyone knew that science knows no boundaries, not even between species!
